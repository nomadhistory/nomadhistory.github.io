Why guests scroll past you — and the one fix that isn't a nicer logo

Twelve places on the same street, all promising clean rooms and a warm welcome. The one that wins isn't the prettiest. It's the one saying something the other eleven can't.

Marina22 July 20267 min read

Open a booking platform for any small town with a bit of tourism and you will see the same paragraph written twelve times. Cosy rooms. Warm, friendly hosts. Great location, close to everything. A breakfast that is, apparently, always generous. Each one is true. Each one is also completely interchangeable — which, to a guest deciding in ninety seconds, is the same as being invisible.

The reflex, when bookings are slow, is to assume the packaging is the problem. New photos. A cleaner logo. A redesign of the site. Sometimes that helps at the margins. But a nicer version of a message nobody can tell apart from eleven others is still a message nobody can tell apart. You do not have a design problem. You have a positioning problem, and it is the more valuable one to solve because almost nobody around you is solving it.

What positioning actually means

Positioning is not a slogan and it is not your values. It is a single, defensible answer to one question a guest is asking whether they say it out loud or not: why should I choose this place instead of the one next door that costs about the same?

The answer has to be something the place next door genuinely cannot claim. "Comfortable and welcoming" fails that test instantly — everyone claims it. "The only guesthouse inside the old tannery walls, run by the family that restored it" cannot be copied, because it is true of exactly one building on earth. That is the difference between decoration and a position.

A position other people can also claim isn't a position. It's a description.

Why it's usually hiding in plain sight

The reason owners rarely land on this themselves is not a lack of imagination. It is proximity. When you have run a place for years, the thing that makes it remarkable has become ordinary to you. The building's history, the reason the road bends the way it does, the dish your grandmother refused to change, the fact that yours is the last house before the trail head — you stopped noticing years ago. To a guest arriving for the first time, any one of those could be the entire reason to book.

So the work is less about invention and more about excavation. You are not making up a story. You are finding the true one that is already there and has been undersold. That is why we spend the first days of every project reading — the region, the records, the building's own past — before anyone opens a design tool.

A quick test you can run today: read your own listing and cross out every sentence that a competitor down the road could paste onto their page word-for-word and have it still be true. Whatever survives is the beginning of your position. If nothing survives, that is the finding.

From a position to a booking

A position is only worth having if it travels all the way into the things a guest actually touches. On its own it changes nothing. In order, it should reshape:

  • The first line they read. The headline on your site and the opening sentence of every channel listing stops describing amenities and starts making the claim only you can make.
  • The photographs. You shoot the thing that proves the position, not another wide shot of a made bed that looks like everyone else's made bed.
  • The price conversation. A place with a clear reason to exist stops competing purely on being the cheapest, because it is no longer being compared like-for-like.
  • Where guests come from. Once the story is legible, your own site and direct channels can carry it — and every booking that arrives direct is one you didn't pay a platform a commission to receive.

None of this requires a bigger budget than the place next door. It requires a sharper answer to the same question everyone is being asked and most are fumbling. The prettiest listing on the street rarely wins. The clearest one does.

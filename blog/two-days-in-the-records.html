Two days in the records before we touch a single pixel

The most valuable part of a rebrand happens in a municipal archive, not in a design tool. Here's what we dig for, and why it decides everything downstream.

Marina10 July 20265 min read

Most studios open a project by opening a design file. We open ours by asking where the town keeps its old paper. It sounds like a detour. It is the opposite: it is the part of the work that makes everything after it faster, cheaper and impossible for a competitor to imitate.

Before we design anything, we spend two days on the ground — in the region, in conversation, and in the records. The reason is simple. A brand built on what the owner remembers off the top of their head tends to arrive at the same three adjectives everyone else used. A brand built on what the place actually is — verifiable, specific, sometimes forgotten even by the people who run it — arrives somewhere nobody nearby can follow.

What we're actually looking for

We are not collecting trivia for a plaque on the wall. We are looking for the handful of true, specific facts that could become the reason a guest chooses this place. In practice that means going through:

  1. The building's own past — what it was before it took guests, who built it, what the walls were doing a hundred years ago.
  2. The region's records — the industry that shaped the town, the route that made it a stopping point, the reason the streets run the way they do.
  3. The first-hand account — the owner's real story, the recipe that never changed, the guest who keeps coming back and why.

Any one of those can turn out to be the whole position. A guesthouse that happens to sit on the last mill in the valley is not a generic guesthouse — it is the last mill in the valley, and no amount of budget lets the place down the road claim the same thing.

Anyone can copy a colour palette. Nobody can copy a hundred years of being the only house on that bend in the road.

Why it pays off later

Research at the start feels slow to a business that wants bookings now. But it is the cheapest step in the whole project, and it removes the most expensive kind of rework — the redesign you commission six months later because the first one looked nice and said nothing.

When the position comes from something real and specific, everything downstream gets easier to make and harder to argue with:

  • The copy writes itself, because you are describing a truth instead of inventing a mood.
  • The photography has a clear subject, because you know exactly what has to be in frame.
  • The whole thing survives being handed over, because it is anchored in facts that don't expire when the trend does.

This is the step almost nobody does, which is exactly why it works. It is also why the research and the build here are done by the same two people — so nothing gets lost in the handoff between someone who read the history and someone who never did.

By the time we open a design tool, the hard decision is already made. The design is just carrying a story we can prove, into the places a guest will actually see it.
